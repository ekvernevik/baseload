1. Price duration curves (NO1–NO5)

What it shows:

* Each zone’s hourly prices sorted from highest to lowest (not in time order).
* Lets you compare overall price level, volatility, extreme spikes, and frequency of low/negative prices.

What to take from it:

* NO1 and NO2 have the highest price levels overall (best curves for value capture).
* NO2 has the largest upside spike (strongest scarcity/extreme-price events).
* NO4 is much lower than the others for most hours (structurally weaker market for arbitrage).
* NO5 sits in the middle; NO3 is below NO1/NO2/NO5 but above NO4 in many hours.
* All zones show some negative-price hours at the far tail.

Implication:

* Storage/arbitrage value should be highest in NO1/NO2 and weakest in NO4.

2. Price heatmap

What it shows:

* Rows are zones (NO1–NO5), columns are hourly timestamps through the year.
* Color intensity indicates price level.

What to take from it:

* Vertical bright bands across multiple rows show system-wide price events affecting several zones at once.
* NO4 is consistently darker for long stretches, confirming lower prices.
* NO1/NO2/NO5 show more frequent high-price periods and more variability.
* The color patterns change by time block/season, indicating regime behavior (not one stationary market pattern).

Implication:

* Supports regime-based forecasting/dispatch rather than one static strategy.

3. Regime calendar

What it shows:

* Each day is assigned to one of 3 clusters/regimes (0, 1, 2) across the year.

What to take from it:

* Regime 2 dominates most of the year.
* Regime 1 is concentrated in early-year and late-year periods (winter-like pattern).
* Regime 0 appears more often in mid-year (summer/autumn), especially around Jul–Oct.

Implication:

* Daily price behavior clearly clusters into recurring seasonal patterns.

4. Regime frequency trend (monthly stacked bars)

What it shows:

* Monthly counts of days in each regime.

What to take from it:

* Regime 2 is the baseline in every month.
* Regime 1 is strongest in Jan–Feb and Nov–Dec, and mostly absent mid-year.
* Regime 0 becomes much more common in summer (notably July) and remains relevant into autumn.

Implication:

* Strong seasonal regime rotation; models/dispatch rules should vary by season/regime.

5. Spread heatmap (top pairs)

What it shows:

* Hourly inter-zone spreads for selected pairs:

  * NO2-NO3
  * NO4-NO5
  * NO3-NO4
  * NO1-NO2

What to take from it:

* NO2-NO3 shows large positive excursions (strong separation episodes).
* NO4-NO5 is frequently negative (NO4 often below NO5).
* NO3-NO4 is often positive (NO3 often above NO4).
* NO1-NO2 looks tighter than the others (smaller spread amplitude overall).

Implication:

* Cross-zonal opportunities are pair-specific; pairs involving NO4 and NO2/NO3 look more structurally interesting than NO1-NO2.

6. PF valuation ranking (€/kW-yr)

What it shows:

* Annualized value under the PF method for each zone, normalized per kW-year.

Ranking (approx):

* NO1 (~47-48 €/kW-yr)
* NO2 (~46 €/kW-yr)
* NO5 (~27-28 €/kW-yr)
* NO3 (~22 €/kW-yr)
* NO4 (~12 €/kW-yr)

What to take from it:

* NO1 and NO2 are clearly the top markets under PF.
* NO4 is much weaker than all others.
* The ranking is consistent with the price distribution plots.

Implication:

* If screening storage locations using PF valuation, prioritize NO1/NO2.

7. PF dispatch profile (NO1, representative week)

What it shows:

* Representative weekly battery operation under PF:

  * SOC (MWh)
  * Charge power (MW)
  * Discharge power (MW)

What to take from it:

* SOC frequently swings between near-min and near-max levels, indicating active cycling.
* Charge/discharge often occurs near apparent power limits (~50 MW), suggesting power-constrained dispatch at times.
* Dispatch is bursty (short charge/discharge episodes), typical of price arbitrage.
* SOC often sits full before discharge windows, implying inventory is held for high-value hours.

Implication:

* PF is using the battery actively and strategically, with both power and energy constraints binding at different times.

8. PF vs RH revenue

What it shows:

* Total revenues under PF and RH methods for each zone.

What to take from it:

* PF and RH revenues are almost identical in every zone (bars visually overlap).
* Same zone ranking as PF valuation:

  * NO1 ≈ NO2 > NO5 > NO3 > NO4

Implication:

* In this setup, PF vs RH choice does not materially affect gross revenue.
* Differences (if any) are likely in operational style, robustness, or implementation complexity rather than topline earnings.

9. RH SOC overlay (NO1, representative week)

What it shows:

* SOC trajectory for RH in the same representative week.

What to take from it:

* The SOC pattern looks very similar to PF (same broad cycling behavior and boundary hits).
* RH also cycles between near-min and near-max levels.

Implication:

* This supports the PF vs RH revenue result: the two approaches produce very similar operational outcomes in this case.

Overall conclusions

* NO1 and NO2 are the best value zones; NO4 is the weakest by a wide margin.
* Prices and spreads exhibit clear seasonality and regime structure, so regime-aware modeling is justified.
* Cross-zonal spread opportunities are not uniform; some pairs (especially involving NO4 and NO2/NO3) are much more interesting than NO1-NO2.
* PF and RH appear nearly equivalent in revenue and similar in dispatch behavior in this analysis, so method choice should be based on practicality/robustness rather than expected gross revenue.
